How words are segmented

Syllables
Split by pronunciation, not spelling. fire is two syllables, /ˈfaɪ.ɚ/. Consonants between vowels go to the following syllable as far as English allows a syllable to begin with them (the maximal onset principle), so monster is /ˈmɑn.stɚ/. A stressed short vowel keeps the next consonant, because English syllables cannot end in one: rabbit is /ˈɹæb.ət/.
Phonemes
Each sound is one phoneme. Affricates /tʃ/ and /dʒ/ and diphthongs /a͡ɪ/, /a͡ʊ/, /ɔ͡ɪ/, /e͡ɪ/, /o͡ʊ/ count as one sound each.
IPA
Broad (phonemic) General American transcription. /ˈ/ marks primary stress and /ˌ/ secondary stress, placed before the syllable. In the syllable boxes, stress is shown by color instead: orange for primary, blue for secondary. Diphthongs carry a tie bar, as in /a͡ɪ/. Stressed and unstressed central vowels are distinguished: /ʌ/ and /ɝ/ when stressed, /ə/ and /ɚ/ when not.
Nonsense words
Made up from sound patterns English allows, with stress usually on the first syllable. Each one is checked against about 250,000 real spellings (including names) and every pronunciation in the CMU dictionary, so it is never a real word, whether written or said aloud. The spelling is only a suggested reading; the IPA is the target.
Sources
Pronunciations from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary. Word list checked against the public-domain Moby Hyphenator to exclude names and abbreviations. Familiarity ranks from FrequencyWords (CC BY-SA 4.0). Dictionary pronunciations can differ from regional speech, so check a word before using it in an assessment.
